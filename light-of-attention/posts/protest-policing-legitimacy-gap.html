Protest Policing and the Legitimacy Gap

Jeremy Says
Police reports and official reviews do not show a clean machine applying equal rules to equal conduct. They show a system classifying crowds before it controls them.
The Light Answers

The argument is not “police are always bad” or “every left protest was peaceful.” The argument is more specific:

Official reports repeatedly show police using broad chemical or less-lethal force against crowds coded as left, racial-justice, anti-police, immigrant-rights, or Palestine-solidarity, while right-wing/pro-police crowds often received more patience, more ambiguity, and more benefit of the doubt. Their own paperwork undermines the neutral crowd-control story.

Philadelphia is the cleanest case because the city’s own investigation directly compared the two treatments. The official review found that Philadelphia police used tear gas on 52nd Street and I-676, including against peaceful protesters and civilians, while a mostly white pro-police Fishtown crowd carrying bats, golf clubs, and other blunt objects was not dispersed with force. The report says the department’s response “varied significantly” and describes “disparate approaches” toward protesters opposing police violence versus groups supporting police. That is the legitimacy gap in official form: anti-police crowd gets gas; pro-police armed crowd gets tolerance.

The I-676 example is especially useful because it breaks the police justification from the inside. The Philadelphia report found that protesters were marching peacefully, that the commissioner had not authorized the CS gas deployment, and that officers continued using gas and rubber bullets as people tried to flee. This matters because the official defense was “public safety,” but the later official review says the force was not justified by the crowd’s actual conduct.

Seattle’s own oversight record is another strong example. Seattle’s Office of Inspector General and Sentinel Event Review materials discuss the use of CS gas, OC spray, and blast balls, and later review material describes what panelists viewed as “wholesale use of force” against a largely peaceful crowd. That phrase is important. If the weapon is indiscriminate, then police are not punishing only the person who did something wrong. They are punishing a crowd category.

Portland gives the same pattern across a longer period. Portland’s official Civilian Review Committee summary records complaints about police deploying tear gas or rubber bullets in response to water bottles, fence-shaking, yelling, or refusal to disperse. It also records complaints that “officer safety” was used to justify serious force even when officers were in heavy protective gear. Portland’s own review says 2020 tactics left many individuals with lasting injuries and trauma and recommends avoiding weapons that target groups rather than individuals when largely peaceful crowds are present.

Denver adds another “their own records are weak” piece. The Denver Office of the Independent Monitor reviewed DPD operational plans, after-action reports, less-lethal munition inventories, and body-worn camera footage. It found serious gaps: the department could not reliably say how many officers were assigned, after-action reports were incomplete, and many officers recorded no body-camera footage during protest assignments. That undercuts the neutral-policing story because the state used force, then lacked the basic records needed to justify it cleanly.

New York’s Department of Investigation makes the legitimacy issue explicit at the level of trust. Its report says protest policing against police misconduct puts public trust and legitimacy on display, and that police must act without bias or misuse of authority. Later summaries of that city investigation describe excessive force, chemical irritants, physical force, and inadequate training. Again, the review does not say, “Everything was normal crowd management.” It says the response damaged legitimacy.

Lafayette Square is useful because even the more police-friendly Interior Department review still gives you damaging facts. It found that not everyone could hear the dispersal warnings; that U.S. Park Police lacked a detailed dispersal-warning policy for that kind of operation; that agencies lacked shared radio communication; that some units deployed before the third warning was complete; that tactics appeared inconsistent with the operational plan; and that MPD did use CS gas on 17th Street. Even where the report rejected the simplest “Trump photo-op caused it” claim, it still showed a chaotic, poorly coordinated force operation against protesters near the White House.

The federal Portland record adds the “outside force” version of the same problem. Congressional Research Service noted lawsuits claiming federal officers attacked journalists and legal observers with tear gas, batons, and rubber bullets. DHS-related reporting said more than 750 federal officers were sent to Portland, and federal officers’ conduct became the subject of watchdog investigations. A later settlement involved claims that federal agents used illegal arrests, tear gas, rubber bullets, and other tactics against racial-justice protesters.

Now put that beside January 6. Official GAO material confirms chemical and less-lethal munitions were part of the Capitol Police preparation and response landscape, but the pro-Trump crowd received that level of response after an extreme escalation: breached police lines, attacks on the Capitol, evacuations, lockdowns, broken windows, and direct danger to Congress. That is not comparable to many of the officially criticized uses of gas against left or racial-justice crowds. The threshold appears much higher before the pro-Trump crowd received the same kind of chemical-force treatment.

The recent anti-ICE protests show this is not only a 2020 story. Reporting from LAPD’s own released numbers says LAPD fired 1,040 projectiles in one day, including CS gas, rubber rounds, and foam rounds, against about 6,000 demonstrators opposing Trump immigration policy and National Guard deployment.

So the expanded finding is:

Their own records show three repeated facts. First, chemical and less-lethal force was often broad, not precise. Second, warnings, documentation, authorization, and command control were often defective. Third, the harshest crowd-control treatment repeatedly fell on movements challenging police violence, racial hierarchy, immigration enforcement, or U.S. policy, while right-wing/pro-police crowds often received more interpretive charity.

That is why “neutral crowd management” fails as the main explanation.

A sharper version:

Police reports and official reviews do not show a clean machine applying equal rules to equal conduct. They show a system classifying crowds before it controls them. Some crowds are presumed civic and familiar; others are presumed disorderly and dangerous. Once the crowd is placed in the disorder category, tear gas becomes easier to justify, even when later reviews show the justification was thin, confused, undocumented, or false.

And the hard comparative sentence:

When a pro-Trump crowd was tear-gassed, it was after a violent breach of the U.S. Capitol. When left, racial-justice, anti-police, immigrant-rights, or Palestine-solidarity crowds were tear-gassed, official records repeatedly show peaceful people, trapped crowds, bystanders, journalists, residents, and lawful protesters being hit too. That is not an equal threshold.
